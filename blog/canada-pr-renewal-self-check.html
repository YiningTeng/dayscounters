Before You Renew Your PR Card, Run This Self-Check

October 29, 2026 · 5 min read · CountToday / ResiDays Insights

The short version

  • IRCC counts the 5 years right before your application — or before the day an officer checks you at the border. You need 730 days in Canada inside that window.
  • You can rebuild the count yourself in one sitting: list every trip, then check the list against your I-94 history, your CBSA record, and your own paper.
  • If the count comes up short, the fix is usually time, not paperwork: stay in Canada and let the window roll until you cross 730 again.

Renewing a PR card feels like a formality. Fill in the form, pay the fee, wait for the plastic. The risk was never the form — it is the count behind it, because a renewal application is one of the moments IRCC actually runs that count.

So run it first, yourself, while the answer is still yours to act on. The same check is worth doing before any long trip out of Canada. It takes one sitting and three steps.

Step 1 — Count the window, not the card

Pick the day you expect to be checked: the day you would file the renewal, or the day you would fly back from a long trip. Count back exactly 5 years from that day. Everything inside that span is the test — not the dates on your card, not the year you landed.

Now list every absence in the window: date out, date back, where you went. One rule works in your favour: if you were in Canada for any part of a day, that day counts as a day in Canada. The day you leave counts. The day you return counts. Only full days outside come off your total.

Days in Canada = the length of the window minus your full days outside. Compare the result with 730. Then be honest about the margin: if you land within about 30 days of the line either way, treat the count as unproven until Step 2 confirms it. Memory is where these counts go wrong.

Been a PR for less than 5 years? Your test is different: can you still reach 730 by your fifth anniversary? Count forward instead — days banked so far, plus every day between now and that anniversary.

Step 2 — Pull the records that prove it

Start with what is already in your hands: passport stamps, boarding passes, booking emails. Lay them against your list from Step 1 and mark every trip that has no paper behind it. Those gaps are what the next two records are for.

I-94 travel history (U.S.). If you cross into the United States, CBP's I-94 site shows your recorded U.S. arrivals and departures. For anyone living a cross-border life, it is the fastest cross-check there is: every U.S. entry is a day you left Canada, and the dates are the government's, not your recollection.

CBSA Traveller History Report. This is Canada's own record of your entries — and your exits too, but only if you ask for exit information specifically. Requesting your own report under the Privacy Act is free, and CBSA says processing can take up to 30 days. Order it while you still have time to spare, not the week you plan to file.

Backfill the rest. Leases, utility bills, pay stubs, tax assessments, bank and card statements with Canadian transactions — paper like this places you in Canada on days when no stamp exists. One more thing: the renewal form lets you consent to IRCC pulling your CBSA report themselves. Fine — but run your own count first. Their copy should not be the first version of your history you ever see.

Step 3 — Check the exceptions before you worry

Some days outside Canada still count toward the 730. The list is short: living abroad with a Canadian citizen spouse or partner (or, for a child, a Canadian citizen parent); working abroad full-time for a Canadian business or a Canadian government that sent you there; or living with a PR spouse or parent who holds that kind of job.

Read it narrowly. Working remotely for a Canadian employer from another country is not the same as being posted abroad by one, and officers ask for proof — an assignment letter, pay records, something with dates. And keep the two rules apart: these exceptions protect PR status only. Citizenship counts days actually spent in Canada, 1,095 of them.

If the count comes up short

First, recount — with the records in hand this time. Most short counts turn out to be record problems: a return stamp nobody made, a day trip nobody wrote down, the any-part-of-a-day rule never applied. A careful recount moves the number more often than people expect.

If the recount still leaves you short and you are in Canada, the move that works is dull: stay, do not file yet, and do not travel. The count heals one day at a time as old absences roll out of the window. Work out the exact date you cross 730 again and plan around it.

Outside Canada and short is the harder spot. To fly back you need a valid PR card or a permanent resident travel document, and the travel document application is itself a residency check. At the border, questions about your status can lead to a section 44 report. Either way: get advice before you move, not after.

And whatever your number is today, start keeping the raw material now — every boarding pass, every booking email, one running list. The next count should take minutes, not an evening of archaeology.

Quick questions

When should I run this check?

Twice: before you file a renewal, and before any long trip if your count is anywhere near the line. The window is measured back from the day of the check, so a count that passes today can fail after one more month abroad.

My passport is missing stamps. Is the count hopeless?

No. Stamps are one record among several: I-94 history, a CBSA Traveller History Report, boarding passes, leases, pay stubs, tax assessments. Build the list from all of them.

Do days with my Canadian spouse abroad count toward citizenship?

No. They can count toward keeping PR. Citizenship needs 1,095 days of physical presence in Canada.
